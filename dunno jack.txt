-   Revise Pulse Code Modulation, specifically how bit depth and sample rate relate to bandwidth.

-   By default, expect 96KHz for recording and 48KHz for playback.

-   Plain vanilla digital audio has no information about audio channels.
    But how do we include channel information? The Pulse Code can itself contain that info!
    The channel count may be specified such as 2:1 which means two channels.
    

-   ALSA is an interface to the kernel's sound driver.
    ALSA takes a Pulse Code PCM signal and sends it to the hardware, through the sound driver.
    
-   Most DACs can only handle one signal at a time (no parallelism).
    What's the solution? Multiplexing. Multiple signals will be mixed somehow, whether in time
    or in frequency. Guess which is more efficient to do?

-   alsalib was the first mixer program. For whatever reason, this was unsatisfying.

-   Pulseaudio is not a driver, nor does it talk to drivers.
    "Driver? Never heard of him"
    It talks to ALSA instead. Your programs talk to PulseAudio and PulseAudio mixes the audio signals
    that they generate.
    Pulse is also nice enough to resample all the audio it receives so that they are in the same format -- same bit-depth, probably channel count too and dynamic range?
    In any case, if one signal is 4-bit audio and the other is 16-bit audio, that can confuse ALSA.
    Pulse handles this somehow by either upsampling the lower-bit signals or downsampling the upper
    bit signals.

-   JACK is also not a driver.
    JACK stands for JACK Audio Connection Kit, and is an audio server.
    Think of it this way - when you walk into a restaurant you don't head to the kitchen asking the
    greasy chef for hamburgers.
    You sit at the table, call the waiter and ask "I'd like hamburgers, nice sir".
    The waiter heads to the kitchen and gives your order to the chef, who makes the hamburgers
    and then gives the waiter the hamburgers, which the waiter delivers to you.
    From your perspective, the kitchen may as well not exist!
    What goes on in the kitchen - the cutting of onions, the frying, etc - is irrelevant.
    You are the client and make a request to the waiter, and the waiter satisfies your request.
    JACk is the waiter, ALSA can be thought of as the Chef at this level of abstraction.
    PulseAudio too is a waiter.
    JACK's emphasis is on low latency and realtime processing. I'm not using the restaurant analogy anymore.
    JACK is quick and responds quickly, but I don't think it can do multiplexing.
    If that's true then it explains my experiences with JACK - whenever I open an application
    such as PureData which uses JACK, I usually can't hear audio from any other source.
    Perhaps mixing adds a new layer of latency? Who knows.

-   Once again, what is ALSA?
    Advanced Linux Sound Architecture. It isn't a driver, but a software architecture consisting of
    kernel drivers, an API and utility programs.
    All this to make sound work properly.
    You can apparently control ALSA directly, hence program sound with it.
    The API library is called 'libasound'
